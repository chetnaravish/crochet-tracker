How to Crochet: Chain Stitch (ch)

The chain is the most basic crochet stitch: you pull a new loop of yarn through the loop already on your hook. A row of chains, called a foundation chain, is the starting point for most flat projects like scarves and blankets.

Chains are also used inside patterns to add height at the start of a row (a turning chain), to make spaces and lacy holes, and to create loops for buttons or hanging.

Step by step

  1. Make a slip knot. Make a loop with the yarn, pull a second loop of the working yarn through it, and put that loop on your hook. Pull the yarn gently to tighten it. ✓ The knot slides on the hook but does not fall off. It is not a chain yet.
  2. Hold the yarn. Hold the hook like a pencil in your writing hand. Drape the working yarn over the index finger of your other hand and pinch the slip knot with that hand’s thumb and middle finger. ✓ The yarn between your finger and the hook is slightly tight.
  3. Yarn over. Move the hook under the yarn and back up, so the yarn wraps over the hook from back to front. ✓ The yarn sits in the hook at the tip.
  4. Pull through. Turn the hook so its tip points down a little and pull the wrapped yarn through the loop on the hook. ✓ 1 loop on your hook and 1 chain (a small “V”) below it.
  5. Repeat. Yarn over and pull through again for every chain your pattern asks for. ✓ Count the V shapes, not the loop on your hook.
  6. Move your fingers up. Every 3 or 4 chains, move your pinching fingers up so they stay close to the hook. ✓ All your chains are about the same size.

US vs UK name

In US patterns this is called Chain (ch). In UK patterns it's Chain (ch). The name is the same in both systems, which makes this one easy.

Where you'll use it

  • Foundation chains for rows
  • Turning chains at the start of rows
  • Chain spaces in lace and granny squares
  • Hanging loops and ties

Common mistakes

  • Chaining too tightly. A tight foundation chain makes the first row hard to work into and pulls the edge in. Try a hook one size bigger for the chain.
  • Counting the loop on the hook. The loop on your hook never counts as a chain; neither does the slip knot.
  • Twisting the chain. When joining a chain into a ring, check it isn't twisted first.

Tip: To count chains, look at the front: each chain is a small “V”. Count the Vs, starting from the one just below your hook.
